Abstract

A monitor that alerts on every difference in a page’s HTML is mostly wrong. We studied 2,104,880 checks made by 3,912 Ember monitors over 90 days. 412,306 checks found a difference in the raw HTML; after removing timestamps, session tokens, ad slots, rotating content and test variants, 61,180 remained. Two reviewers labelled a random 2,400 of those, and 2,100 (87.5%) were changes a person would want to hear about. Alerting on the raw difference would have had a precision of 13.0%; alerting after normalisation, 87.5%.

1. Introduction

People watch pages to learn when something they care about moves: a competitor’s price, a changelog, a job posting, a clause in a policy. The simplest monitor fetches the page on a schedule and compares it with the last copy. The trouble is that most pages differ on every fetch for reasons nobody cares about.1

This paper measures how much of that difference is noise, where it comes from, and how much of it a normalisation step removes before anyone is alerted.

2. Data

We used every scheduled check made by monitors on paid Ember teams between July 1 and September 28, 2026: 2,104,880 checks of 3,912 pages, made every 6 hours to every 7 days. Teams on the Free plan and teams with zero data retention on were left out, since we keep no page content for them.2

Data and method

The labelled sample, the normalisation rules and the scripts that produce every figure here are published with the paper. Page content is not: we release hashes of each diff and its label.

3. Method

Each check that differed from the previous one went through five rules, in order, and we counted what each rule removed. A diff that survived every rule counted as a change. From the survivors we drew 2,400 at random, stratified by page type, and two reviewers labelled each as meaningful or not.3

4. Results

Of the 2,400 labelled diffs, 2,100 were meaningful: precision after normalisation is 87.5%. Applied to all 61,180 survivors, that is about 53,500 changes worth an alert, against 412,306 raw differences: a raw-diff monitor would have been right 13.0% of the time.

Pages that publish on purpose change most. A changelog had a meaningful change in 9.8% of its checks; a product page, in 2.6%.

The 300 survivors labelled noise were mostly reordered lists (41%) and prices shown in the visitor’s currency (27%).4 Both are now rules in Ember’s ignore presets.

5. Limitations

Monitors are chosen by people who expect a page to change, so these pages change more than a random page would. Reviewers judged meaning without knowing why each monitor was set up; a change one team ignores may be the one another team watches for.

Notes

  1. A check is one fetch of one URL by one monitor. A diff is a check whose content differs from the previous check of the same monitor.
  2. Zero data retention keeps no request bodies or page content, so those checks cannot be compared after the fact. 418 monitors were left out for this reason.
  3. The reviewers disagreed on 74 of 2,400 diffs (3.1%); a third reviewer decided those.
  4. 123 and 81 of the 300 diffs labelled noise.
